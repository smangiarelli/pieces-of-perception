Section 09 · Trigger Word Replacements

The Specific Word Swaps

Live, named substitutions. Every adult he sees — including extended family and visiting friends — needs the swap list.

Retire  ·  why it matters
Retire “STOP” → use “Pause / Wait / Hold on / Freeze”
STOP is the live trigger word. It pulls him back into the PTSD body memory. Replace it everywhere — every adult, every context, every visitor.
Retire “Red is bad / green is good” → use “Red is the color of fire trucks”
Synesthesia means colors have feelings. Moralizing a color tells him it has bad feelings, which distresses him.
Retire “Calm down” → use “Take a breath with me”
Calm cannot be commanded. Co-regulating models it.
Retire “That’s not real” → use “I’m not going to feed that thought or worry”
Don’t dismiss the thoughts. Don’t engage them either. Acknowledge without confirming.
Retire “You’re fine” → use “That was hard. You bounced back.”
Don’t minimize. Affirm the difficulty AND the recovery.

Section 02 · Quick Reference

Jamie at a Glance

A 1-page card. Print it, post it on the fridge, hand it to anyone new — babysitter, sub provider, ER triage nurse.

The card  ·  as it prints
What regulates him
Hugs — he asks many times a day, even when not visibly upset; honor every request. Books. Music of all kinds. Walks. Car rides. An adult getting down to his eye level.
What overwhelms him
The energetic complexity of crowded spaces (not volume — energy). Strong smells. Stuck thoughts comparing two unrelated things. Frustrated adult expressions. The word “STOP.”
Early warning signs
Verbal stimming and looping phrases. The rhythmic breathing sound — this is a self-regulation attempt, a milestone, let it happen. Asking trick questions of people he doesn’t know well.
What never to say
“STOP.” “Because I said so.” Anything in a scolding tone. Color-as-moral framing. Avoid visible frustration — he won’t hear the words past your face.

Section 07 · Implementation Framework

Your 12-Week Map

Built around the three goals you named. Each phase has one primary lever — never more.

Weeks 1–2 · Language reset
The whole household removes “STOP” from active vocabulary. Color-as-moral framing is retired. “I'm not going to feed that thought or worry” becomes the family-wide phrase.

Expect: lots of accidental “STOP”s in week one. Self-correct calmly and move on.
Weeks 3–4 · Build trust in adults outside the household
Name the three adults he already trusts as “Jamie's team” out loud. Brief every new provider on the honor-the-lean rule. Add no new adults this phase — stability over breadth.

Expect: elevated behaviors at the start of any new session. Resist “how was it?” right afterward.
Weeks 5–6 · Soften the rigidity loop
Not challenging the synesthesia or the categorical thinking — introducing “Two things can be true at once” as a frame. When a stuck thought arrives, don't argue: “Tell me more — and what's happening in your body right now?”

Weeks 7–12 continue through confidence-building, sleep and the puberty conversation — each with actions and what to expect.

Section 01 · Executive Summary

The Whole Picture

How to use this report
Read sections 1–3 first. Section 4 (Communication) is the section to copy and hand to anyone new. The Reference Guide near the end is the single page you can print for the fridge. The Appendix is a verbatim copy of every answer you shared — so Jamie's story doesn't have to be told from scratch every time.

The current pressure point is the collision between rigid black-and-white thinking and social anxiety: thought patterns pull toward 'trick questions' and categorical judgments, which can antagonize the very people whose approval he then anxiously seeks.

Three anchor truths to start with

Strengths first. Curiosity, memory, and love of music, animals and books are the operating system. Every strategy builds on those — never around them.
Calm tone before words. His nervous system locks onto the adult's emotional state before the content lands. If you are frustrated, he won't hear the sentence — he'll only see your face.
Honor the lean. He rarely greets new adults warmly, and he WILL test the situation. If he gives a lean, comes close, or makes any move toward you — he is starting to trust. That is the win.
